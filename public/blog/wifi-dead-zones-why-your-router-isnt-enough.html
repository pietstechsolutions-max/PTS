Wi-Fi Dead Zones: Why Your Router Isn't Enough

Why one router can't cover a Long Island or Hudson Valley home, why mesh kits disappoint, and what a real whole-home Wi-Fi upgrade looks like.

Published August 25, 2026 · 3 min read · by Piets Technology Solutions

The Wi-Fi is perfect in the kitchen and useless in the upstairs bedroom. The basement office drops Zoom calls. The patio gets one bar. You've rebooted the router, moved it, maybe bought the "long range" one. Still dead zones.

Here's the honest explanation — and the fix that actually works.

One router was never going to cover the house

A single router is a single radio sitting in one spot, usually wherever the cable company left it: a corner of the basement or a closet by the front door. Wi-Fi signal drops off fast with distance and falls apart when it hits:

  • Plaster and lath walls in older Long Island and Hudson Valley homes
  • Brick, stone and stucco exteriors
  • Floors — especially with radiant heat or tile
  • Metal: ductwork, mirrors, appliances, foil-backed insulation
  • Water: fish tanks, water heaters, and the hot tub on the deck

Signal that has to pass through two floors and three walls arrives weak, and weak signal means slow speeds, buffering and dropped calls — even if you're paying for gigabit internet. Speed at the modem is not speed in the bedroom.

Why "just get mesh" often disappoints

Mesh kits from the big-box store solve the problem on paper: put a few pods around the house and they talk to each other. In practice, two things go wrong.

They talk to each other over Wi-Fi. Every hop from pod to pod cuts your speed roughly in half, and the pods have to be close enough to hear each other — which, in a house with dead zones, is exactly the problem you started with.

They get placed wherever there's an outlet. On a dresser, behind a couch, next to the microwave. Access points work best on a ceiling or high on a wall with nothing around them.

Mesh isn't bad. Mesh with wired backhaul, placed properly, is excellent. That's the difference between a kit and a network.

What a real fix looks like

A whole-home Wi-Fi upgrade done by someone who does this for a living looks like this:

  1. A survey. We walk the house with a signal meter and map where it drops and why. Ten minutes, and it changes the whole plan.
  2. Wired access points. One Cat6 cable runs to each access point — typically 2 to 4 for a Long Island colonial, more for a Hudson Valley farmhouse with an outbuilding. The cable carries power (PoE), so there's no outlet needed and no pod on the dresser.
  3. Ceiling or wall mounting, central to the area each one covers. Clean, low-profile, looks like a smoke detector.
  4. One network name everywhere. Your phone hands off from one access point to the next as you walk, without dropping the call.
  5. A proper router and switch. The cable-company box becomes just a modem. A real router handles guest networks, smart home devices on their own network, and parental controls that actually work.
  6. Outdoor coverage for the patio, pool or backyard if you want it — a weatherproof access point under the eave covers most yards.

What it costs, honestly

It costs more than an off-the-shelf mesh kit and usually far less than people fear. The main variables are how many access points the house needs and how hard the cable runs are — a finished basement and attic access make it easy; a slab-on-grade ranch with a finished ceiling takes more work. We quote the exact gear and labor up front, and we'll show you a demo of the difference on a video call if you want to see it before deciding.

Quick self-check before you call

  • Does the signal drop in the same rooms every time? (Coverage problem — access points fix it.)
  • Is it fine on the phone but bad on the laptop? (Old laptop Wi-Fi card — cheap fix.)
  • Is everything slow at 8 pm? (Congestion or an ISP problem, not coverage.)
  • Do you have more than 25 devices? (Smart home gear overwhelming a consumer router.)
